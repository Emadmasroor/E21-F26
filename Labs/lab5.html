WARNING Disclaimer: This section and the next one assume some knowledge of linear algebra, specifically properties of dot products of vectors. If you haven't seen the relevant course material, no worries! The important take-away from the discussion below is that **finding the intersection point of a view ray and an analytic surface such as a sphere or a torus can be accomplished by finding the roots of a particular polynomial.** In computer graphics, [*ray tracing*]("https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ray_tracing_(graphics)") refers to the process of image formation by constructing a series of geometric rays originating from a simulated camera that pass through the center of pixel locations on a virtual image plane in front of the camera, as shown in the diagram below. ![Figure [rtwiki]: diagram showing image formation via construction of view rays (source: [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Ray_trace_diagram.svg))](images/raytrace-wikipedia.png style="width: 75%") Each ray is specified by its origin vector $\mathbf{r}_o \in \mathbb{R}^3$ and its direction vector $\mathbf{r}_d \in \mathbb{R}^3$. In general, all rays share a common origin which is the position of the eye or the camera; however, each ray has its own unique ray direction determined by its pixel location in the image. The position of any point $\mathbf{p} \in \mathbb{R}^3$ along a given ray is given by the parametric function $\mathbf{p}(t) = \mathbf{r}_o + t \, \mathbf{r}_d$, or to examine the elements of each vector individually: $$ \left[ \begin{array}{c} p_x \\ p_y \\ p_z \end{array} \right] = \left[ \begin{array}{c} o_x \\ o_y \\ o_z \end{array}\right] + t \, \left[ \begin{array}{c} d_x \\ d_y \\ d_z \end{array}\right].$$ Equivalently, you can conceptualize the vector equation above as three coupled scalar functions $$ \begin{align*} p_x(t) & = o_x + t \, d_x \\ p_y(t) & = o_y + t \, d_y \\ p_z(t) & = o_z + t \, d_z. \\ \end{align*} $$ In either case, the parameter $t$ corresponds to the distance traveled along the ray. When $t = 0$, the point is at the origin, and as $t \rightarrow \infty$, the point moves further and further along the ray away from the origin in the direction of travel. Determining whether each ray hits a primitive (such as a sphere or a torus), and which surface has been hit first, can be accomplished by finding the roots of a particular function of $t$. In many cases, graphics primitives can be represented as [implicit functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Implicit_function) where the surface of the object is the set of all points $\mathbf{p}$ such that $f(\mathbf{p}) = 0$. For example, a sphere centered at the origin with radius $r$ is the set of points $(x, y, z)$ such that $$ x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = r^2.$$ We can define this as an implicit function by moving the radius term to the left hand side: $$ f(x, y, z) = x^2 + y^2 + z^2 - r^2 = 0. $$ We can also write this in vector form: $$ f(\mathbf{p}) = \| \mathbf{p} \|^2 - r^2 = \mathbf{p} \bullet \mathbf{p} - r^2 = 0. $$ What's the connection to root finding? Let's substitute in our parametric equation for $\mathbf{p}(t)$ from above, and expand: $$ \begin{align*} 0 & = \mathbf{p(t)} \bullet \mathbf{p(t)} - r^2 \\ & = (\mathbf{r}_o + t \, \mathbf{r}_d ) \bullet (\mathbf{r}_o + t \, \mathbf{r}_d ) - r^2. \end{align*} $$ Owing to the distributive and commutative products of the dot product, we can further transform this equation: $$ \begin{align*} 0 & = \mathbf{r}_o \bullet \mathbf{r}_o + \mathbf{r}_o \bullet ( t \, \mathbf{r}_d ) + ( t \, \mathbf{r}_d ) \bullet \mathbf{r}_o + (t \, \mathbf{r}_d) \bullet (t \, \mathbf{r}_d) - r^2 \\ & = \mathbf{r}_o \bullet \mathbf{r}_o + t \, \mathbf{r}_o \bullet \mathbf{r}_d + t \, \mathbf{r}_d \bullet \mathbf{r}_o + t^2 \, \mathbf{r}_d \bullet \mathbf{r}_d - r^2 \\ & = \mathbf{r}_o \bullet \mathbf{r}_o + t \, \mathbf{r}_o \bullet \mathbf{r}_d + t \, \mathbf{r}_o \bullet \mathbf{r}_d + t^2 \, \mathbf{r}_d \bullet \mathbf{r}_d - r^2 \\ & = \mathbf{r}_o \bullet \mathbf{r}_o + 2 t \, \mathbf{r}_o \bullet \mathbf{r}_d + t^2 \, \mathbf{r}_d \bullet \mathbf{r}_d - r^2. \end{align*} $$ Grouping terms by order of exponent in $t$, we arrive at the quadratic equation $at^2 + b t + c = 0$, where $$ \begin{align*} a & = \mathbf{r}_d \bullet \mathbf{r}_d \\ b & = 2 \mathbf{r}_o \bullet \mathbf{r}_d \\ c & = \mathbf{r}_o \bullet \mathbf{r}_o - r^2. \end{align*} $$ Note that a given ray can intersect a sphere at 0 points (misses the sphere), 1 point (touches the sphere at a tangent point) or 2 points (goes into, and then out of the sphere). Thes correspond to the quadratic polynomial having 0, 1, or 2 real roots, respectively. In the case of multiple real roots, we are always concerned with finding the one that has the minimum $t$ because this is the first point on the sphere that will be visible along the ray. The implicit equation for a torus with minor radius $r_1$ and major radius $r_2$ is given by $$ \left( r_2 - \sqrt{x^2 + y^2} \right)^2 + z^2 - r_1^2 = 0.$$ By an analogous procedure to a sphere, the ray-torus intersection problem can be converted to finding the smallest real root of a quartic (fourth-degree) polynomial. !!! Tip Numpy provides polynomial root finding via [**`np.polynomial.polynomial.polyroots`**](https://numpy.org/doc/stable/reference/generated/numpy.polynomial.polynomial.polyroots.html). Note that it expects coefficients to be provided in **increasing order of exponent**, with constant term first, so that the coefficients for the quadratic above would be specified as `[c, b, a]`. You will replace the starter code's calls to the numpy solver with your own implementation of the Durand-Kerner method. ## Durand-Kerner method The Durand-Kerner method is an iterative root finding technique for polynomials. It uses complex arithmetic, and can find both the real roots and the complex roots of a given polynomial. You will implement this method to find the roots of quadratic and quartic polynomials that arise in raytracing (see section below). Let $f(x)$ be a degree $n$ polynomial with $n + 1$ coefficients. The Durand-Kerner method maintains a list of $n$ root estimates $x_1$ to $x_n$. Since it is an iterative method, we will denote the estimate for the $k^\text{th}$ root at the $i^\text{th}$ iteration as $x_{k,i}$. The update rule for Durand-Kerner is given by $$ x_{k,i+1} = x_{k,i} - \frac{f(x_{k,i})}{\displaystyle \prod_{j < k}( x_{k,i} - x_{j,i+1} ) \cdot \prod_{j > k}( x_{k,i} - x_{j,i} ) }. $$ To provide a concrete example with $n = 3$, we would start with initial root estimates $x_{1,1}$, $x_{2,1}$, and $x_{3,1}$, then use the updates $$ \begin{align*} x_{1,2} & = x_{1,1} - \frac{f(x_{1,1})}{(x_{1,1} - x_{2,1})(x_{1,1} - x_{3,1})} \\ \\ x_{2,2} & = x_{2,1} - \frac{f(x_{2,1})}{(x_{2,1} - x_{1,2})(x_{2,1} - x_{3,1})} \\ \\ x_{3,2} & = x_{3,1} - \frac{f(x_{3,1})}{(x_{3,1} - x_{1,2})(x_{3,1} - x_{2,2})}. \end{align*} $$ !!! Tip Given a point $x$ and coefficients $c_k$ in increasing order of exponent (i.e. constant term first), you can evaluate a polynomial using [**`numpy.polynomial.polynomial.polyval`**](https://numpy.org/doc/stable/reference/generated/numpy.polynomial.polynomial.polyval.html). Iteration should continue until the approximate relative error $\varepsilon_{k,i}$ for all root estimates $x_{k,i}$ is below a specified tolerance. The approximate relative error is given by $$ \varepsilon_{k,i} = \frac{|x_{k,i} - x_{k,i-1}|}{|x_{k,i}|}. $$ For further information, I suggest you check out the [Wikipedia page on Durand-Kerner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Durand%E2%80%93Kerner_method). !!! Warning When implementing Durand-Kerner, you do not need to store a 2D array of root estimates (e.g. for all $k$ and $i$) -- a 1D array (over $k$) is sufficient. I suggest you use a set of nested **`for`** loops that updates each root over each each iteration. You may need to use a temporary variable to store each new root estimate before you compute approximate relative error and then update the array with the new root estimate. ## Raymarching One alternative method to root finding on polynomials is raymarching, also known as sphere tracing. Raymarching is an iterative process that updates a current estimate of the ray intersection point over time. The initial estimate is placed at or close to the ray origin $\mathbf{r}_o$. Then, at each iteration, the distance from the current estimated intersection point to the nearest feature in the scene is computed. The next update is obtained by marching that distance forward along the ray from the current position, as shown in this animation by Inigo Quilez: ![Figure [raymarch]: graphical diagram illustrating raymarching in action (source: [Inigo Quilez via Internet Archive](https://archive.org/details/pdfy-UeAq0gzWykMFwt2T))](images/lab5_anim.gif style="width: 75%") In raymarching, the ray direction vector is typically normalized to have unit length, so that $\| \mathbf{r}_d \| = 1$. Then the update rule is given by $$ t_\text{new} = t_\text{old} + \mathrm{dist}\big(\mathbf{p}(t_\text{old})\big) = t_\text{old} + \mathrm{dist}\big( \mathbf{r}_o + t_\text{old} \, \mathbf{r}_d \big) $$ where $\mathrm{dist}(\mathbf{p})$ is the function that computes the distance from the point $\mathbf{p}$ to the nearest point in the scene. Iteration continues until the distance is within a preset tolerance (i.e. a true intersection has been reached) or until a preset number of iterations has been reached (at which point the ray is presumed to have missed the objects in the scene). Inigo Quilez maintains a [web page](https://iquilezles.org/articles/distfunctions/) listing many helpful examples of commonly used distance functions. Note that the code examples on that page are provided in GL Shading Language (GLSL), which has slightly different syntax than the Python starter code that I provide. ## Lighting and shadowing Once a ray intersection point has been determined, [additional calculations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_graphics_lighting) can be performed to determine the appearance of the surface at that point. These usually depend on the [surface normal]("https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normal_(geometry)") at the intersection point -- that is, the direction of the vector that is perpendicular to the object at the point of intersection. The surface normal can be obtained from an implicit function representation of the surface when raytracing, or by evaluating the gradient (first derivative) of the distance function when raymarching. Shadowing can be computed by casting another ray from the intersection point to nearby light sources. If the ray can travel uninterrupted to the light source without intersecting another object, the original intersection point is lit; otherwise, it is in shadow.
